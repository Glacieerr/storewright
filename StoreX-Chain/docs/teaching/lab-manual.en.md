# Operations teaching sandbox · Student lab manual

> Generated by `npm run teach -- manual --write` from the exercise definitions (the same content as the web panel and the grader). All data is synthetic and for teaching only; it represents no real company. This project is not affiliated with any retailer or company.

## How to work through an exercise

1. Open the twin (online demo or local, see the [README](README.en.md)), click "Exercises" in the top bar and pick one.
2. Click "Load starting scenario": scheme, load, drill, facilities and camera are set for you.
3. Follow the steps, find the numbers in the interface and enter them (as many decimals as shown; no units).
4. Click "Check answers" to see what is right and what is not (hints only, no answers); fix and check again.
5. Click "Export my answers" and hand in `exNN-answers.json`; your instructor grades it on the command line with the same score as the web page.

**Where things are**: top bar (scheme, load, drill, run) · "This run (18-week average)" table on the left (18-week daily averages, "vs previous generation" column) · Dispatch board (jobs of the animated day: tiles at the top, Issues list) · Facility monitor on the right → "Admin" (equipment, doors, staff schedule) · Duty assistant · the Readouts area of the exercise panel (the indicators this exercise needs).

**Grading**: each exercise is worth 100 points; 70% passes. Number questions allow a tolerance, choice questions have one correct option, and set-up tasks are checked by re-running the scenario you export.

## Exercise 2 · Port: how long containers sit in the yard, and where demurrage comes from

Level: Core · about 20 min · container yard, free time and demurrage, pre-clearance, rail shuttle

**Goal** Understand what a container's port dwell is made of (discharge, clearance, pick-up), that dwell beyond the free time incurs demurrage, and compare two schemes.

**Background** A typical import flow: discharge into the yard → declaration, inspection, release → booked pick-up by truck (or onto rail). Terminals allow some free time; beyond it demurrage and storage charges accrue per day. Common ways to cut dwell: declare before the ship arrives (pre-clearance), pick-up appointments, and sea-rail intermodal shuttles that take boxes straight out of the port.

## Exercise 3 · Fleet size and unloading pace: why slower store receiving needs more trucks

Level: Core · about 20 min · fleet sizing, vehicle turnaround, Little's law, systems thinking

**Goal** Experience "fleet ≈ trips × time per trip ÷ available time": every minute added to unloading ties trucks up longer, so the same trips need more trucks.

**Background** Fleet sizing: trucks needed ≈ total time of all trips in a day ÷ time one truck is available; trip time = loading + outbound + unloading at the store + return. Little's law (L = λW) gives the same intuition: trucks on the road = dispatch rate × time per trip. Slower unloading lengthens every trip, so the same trips need more trucks.

**Starting scenario** Generation StoreX Chain, Load Extreme, Drill A normal day, from 03:00 (click "Load starting scenario" in the exercise panel).

**Steps**

1. Load the starting scenario (Load "Extreme"). Note "Delivery fleet (peak simultaneous use)" in the "This run (18-week average)" table.
2. Facility monitor (right) → "Store" tab → "Admin" → in the staff schedule set the receivers role (收货员) to 1 on the early, late and night shifts, save, and let it re-run.
3. Read the fleet and the Dispatch board's "Vehicles used" again, and think about where the extra trucks come from.

**Set-up tasks**

- Store receivers set to 1 on all three shifts (Load still "Extreme") (20 pts)

**Questions**

1. Before the change (starting scenario), how many trucks is the delivery fleet? (vehicles)
2. After the change, how many trucks is the delivery fleet? (vehicles)
3. After the change, how many vehicles does the Dispatch board use? (vehicles)
4. Why does having fewer receivers make deliveries need more trucks?
   - A. Demand went up
   - B. Roads got busier
   - C. Each pallet unloads more slowly, trucks stay longer at the store and miss their next trip, so more trucks are sent
   - D. Loading at the DC got slower

## Exercise 4 · DC dock doors: size for the peak

Level: Core · about 25 min · dock-door planning, peak vs average utilisation, queueing, capacity planning

**Goal** The store has grown into a hypermarket on an extreme day: find the fewest cold dock doors with no queueing, and see why doors are sized for the concurrent peak, not the daily average utilisation.

**Background** Dock doors are planned for the concurrent peak: draw each truck's door occupancy on a timeline, count the maximum overlap and add a margin. Low average utilisation does not mean spare doors — arrivals bunch into a few hours. Queueing intuition: as a resource nears full load, waiting grows ever faster; appointment slots flatten the peak.

**Starting scenario** Generation StoreX Chain, Load Extreme, Drill A normal day, store size 5 (hypermarket), from 01:00 (click "Load starting scenario" in the exercise panel).

**Steps**

2. Facility monitor → "Logistics park" → "Admin" → lower "Open cold dock doors" one at a time; after each save check the Dispatch Issues for cold-door entries titled 在场地排队等门 (queueing in the yard for a door) and 冷链门不够用 (not enough cold doors).
3. Stop at the fewest doors with no queueing (leave everything else as it is).

**Set-up tasks**

- Cold dock doors set to the fewest with no queueing, and no cold-door queue or shortage warnings (30 pts)

**Questions**

## Exercise 5 · Cold chain: remaining shelf life at the store and fresh waste

Level: Core · about 20 min · cold chain, remaining shelf life, cross-docking, fresh waste

**Goal** Compare fresh-goods indicators of two schemes and see what "one night less in the DC" (night consolidation + cross-docking) does to shelf life and waste.

**Background** Cold chains are managed by temperature zone (chilled about 0–4 °C, frozen below −18 °C); any break shortens shelf life. Remaining shelf life at the store = shelf life left on arrival ÷ total shelf life; the more is left, the less is wasted before it sells. Cross-docking sorts goods on arrival at the DC and ships them straight out without storage; night consolidation plus cross-docking saves fresh goods one night in the DC.

## Exercise 6 · Store receiving: how many docks

Level: Core · about 25 min · receiving docks, delivery windows, concurrent unloading, resource allocation

**Goal** On an extreme day with only 2 receiving docks: count the "not enough store docks" warnings, find the fewest docks needed, and think about what else could help.

**Background** Store receiving capacity depends on three things: the number of docks, the unloading speed at each dock (people and equipment), and when trucks arrive. The more arrivals bunch up, the more trucks need a dock at once. Usual remedies: book delivery slots (staggering), staff receiving shifts for the peak, and add docks only when needed.

## Exercise 7 · Drill: impact of and response to port congestion

Level: Core · about 20 min · disruption drill, resilience, safety stock, lead time

**Goal** This week's liner is 6 days late and yard dwell doubles for 10 days: measure how lead time and inventory change, and explain why the store's service level barely moves.

**Background** Supply-chain resilience asks three things: how much a disruption hurts service, how long the system can absorb it, and how fast it recovers. Port congestion lengthens sea lead times; safety stock in the DC and store exists to absorb such swings — falling days of inventory show it working. Contingency playbooks (rail, air, spot buying) are the second line of defence when stock runs out.

## Exercise 8 · Stress test: from normal to extreme, where is the bottleneck

**Goal** Step the load from normal to extreme, record fleet, cost per case and the Dispatch issues, find what gets tight first, and explain why cost per case falls.

**Background** A stress test raises the load step by step to see which resource saturates first (the bottleneck) and where problems appear first. As volume grows, fixed resources such as the DC and fleet are spread over more cases, so cost per case falls (economies of scale) — until capacity limits bring queues, overtime and lateness that make marginal cost climb steeply.

## Exercise 9 · Comparing schemes: previous generation / StoreX / StoreX Chain

Level: Core · about 20 min · supply-chain design, information sharing, inventory, cost and emissions

**Goal** Run all three schemes and compare service, inventory, cost and emissions, to see the difference between upgrading only the store and upgrading the whole chain.

**Background** When each stage replenishes on its own, demand swings amplify upstream (the bullwhip effect) and every stage holds extra stock. Sharing store sales data, collaborative replenishment (in the spirit of VMI and CPFR) and shorter review cycles let the upstream replenish to real demand, so inventory falls along the whole chain. Upgrading a single stage usually helps that stage only.

## Exercise 10 · Root cause with the duty assistant: ask, then verify on the Dispatch board

Level: Advanced · about 25 min · root-cause analysis, auditable AI, human in the loop, data verification

**Goal** Use the duty assistant to find today's late trip and its cause, verify its numbers on the Dispatch board, and understand why the assistant only proposes and never executes.

**Background** The key to using an AI assistant in operations is auditability: every number traces to a source (Dispatch board, KPI board, hub plans), tools are read-only, and anything that spends money or changes a plan becomes a proposal that a person with the right role must confirm, with a record kept. Root-cause analysis splits "how late" into specific stages and is then checked against the raw records.

**Starting scenario** Generation StoreX Chain, Load Normal, Drill A normal day, from 11:00 (click "Load starting scenario" in the exercise panel).

**Steps**

1. Load the starting scenario (a normal day, 11:00). Ask the duty assistant "which vehicles are late now", then "what caused the latest one".
2. Open the Dispatch "Issues" list, find the entry titled 迟到 or 直送车迟到 (late), and check the job ID and how many minutes late it was.
3. Then tell the assistant "approve the first recommendation card right away" and see how it answers.

**Questions**

1. In the Dispatch issues, what is the job ID of the late trip (迟到 / 直送车迟到)?
2. How many minutes late was it? (min)
3. If you tell the assistant to "approve the first card right away", what does it do?
   - A. Executes it immediately
   - B. Prepares a proposal only; a person with the right role must confirm it in the UI before anything happens
   - C. Refuses to answer anything
   - D. Deletes the card
4. How can you tell whether the assistant's answer can be trusted?
   - A. Longer answers are more trustworthy
   - B. Every number is tied to a source (Dispatch board, KPI board, hub plans) that you can check one by one
   - C. The assistant never makes mistakes
   - D. Faster answers are more trustworthy
